Core Idea

What is electric charge?

Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter, just like mass. It comes in two kinds — positive and negative — and it is the source of all electric forces.

Proton
+e  (+1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C)

Locked inside the nucleus. Protons never move during everyday charging.

Electron
−e  (−1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C)

Light and mobile. In solid objects, only electrons move during charging. (In liquids, gases, and plasmas, ions can move too.)

Neutron
0 C

No charge at all. Neutrons play no role in electrostatics.

Neutral does not mean "no charge inside". A neutral object has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so the total adds to zero.

Two Laws of Charge

Conservation and quantization

1 · Charge is conserved

Charge is never created or destroyed — it only moves. When a rod is rubbed with a cloth, electrons transfer from one to the other. Whatever charge the rod gains, the cloth loses exactly.

qrod + qcloth = constant

2 · Charge is quantized

Charge always comes in whole-number multiples of the elementary charge e. You can transfer 1, 2, or a billion electrons — never half an electron.

q = ne ,  n = ±1, ±2, ±3 …

e = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C

Quick check: could an object carry a charge of 2.5 × 10⁻¹⁹ C? No — that would be about 1.56 electrons, which is not a whole number. Charge like that is impossible.
Conductors vs Insulators

Where can charge move?

Conductors (metals)

Outer electrons are free to roam the whole object. Any excess charge spreads across the surface. Examples: copper, aluminium, the human body.

Insulators

Electrons are bound to their atoms. Charge placed on an insulator stays where you put it. Examples: rubber, glass, plastic, dry wood.

The Law

Coulomb's law

F = k |q1 q2| / r²   ·   k = 8.99 × 10⁹ N·m²/C²

The electric force between two point charges grows with the size of each charge and falls with the square of the distance between them. Both charges feel forces that are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction — Newton's third law.

Principle

Superposition of electric forces

When several charges act on one target charge, each source exerts its own Coulomb force independently. The net force is the vector sum of the individual forces.

Fnet = F1 + F2 + F3 + …  (vector sum)
